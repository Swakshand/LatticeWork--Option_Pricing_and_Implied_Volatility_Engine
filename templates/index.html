The mathematics

No-arbitrage & the risk-neutral measure

Over one step the option payoff can be replicated by holding \(\Delta\) shares and some cash. No-arbitrage forces today's option value to equal the cost of that replicating portfolio, which turns out to be a discounted expectation under a measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) in which the stock drifts at the risk-free rate:

\[ V_0 \;=\; e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\,\text{payoff}(S_T)\,\right]. \]

CRR parameters

With \(\Delta t = T/N\), the Cox–Ross–Rubinstein choice is

\[ u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = \frac{1}{u} = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d}. \]

The condition \(ud = 1\) makes the tree recombine, so after \(i\) steps there are only \(i+1\) distinct prices \(S_0\,u^{\,j} d^{\,i-j}\). The probability \(p\) is chosen so the discounted stock price is a martingale: \(pu + (1-p)d = e^{r\Delta t}\).

Backward induction

Set terminal values to the payoff, then roll back:

\[ V_i^{\,j} = e^{-r\Delta t}\Big(p\,V_{i+1}^{\,j+1} + (1-p)\,V_{i+1}^{\,j}\Big). \]

American early exercise

At every node compare the continuation value above against immediate exercise, and keep the larger:

\[ V_i^{\,j} = \max\!\Big(\underbrace{\text{payoff}(S_i^{\,j})}_{\text{exercise}},\; \underbrace{e^{-r\Delta t}\big(p\,V_{i+1}^{\,j+1} + (1-p)\,V_{i+1}^{\,j}\big)}_{\text{hold}}\Big). \]

Convergence & the sawtooth

As \(N \to \infty\) the binomial price converges to Black–Scholes. The error decays like \(\mathcal{O}(1/N)\) but oscillates: whether a terminal node sits just above or below the strike \(K\) flips as \(N\) increments, shifting the payoff mass near the money and producing the characteristic even/odd sawtooth.

Implied volatility & the smile

Black–Scholes price is a strictly increasing function of \(\sigma\) (its Vega is always \(\ge 0\)), so for any price inside the no-arbitrage bounds there is exactly one \(\sigma\) that reproduces it. Implied volatility is that \(\sigma\), solved backwards from a real quoted price \(V_{\text{mkt}}\):

\[ \sigma_{\text{implied}} : \quad \text{BS}(S, K, r, \sigma_{\text{implied}}, T) = V_{\text{mkt}}. \]

We solve this with Newton–Raphson: start from a rule-of-thumb guess, then repeatedly step \(\sigma \leftarrow \sigma - \dfrac{\text{BS}(\sigma) - V_{\text{mkt}}}{\text{Vega}(\sigma)}\), using the model's own analytic Vega as the local slope. Far out-of-the-money options have almost zero Vega, which makes Newton's step unstable there — so we detect that and fall back to plain bisection (slower, but guaranteed to converge once a root is bracketed). Prices that sit outside the model-free no-arbitrage bounds \([\max(S-Ke^{-rT},0),\,S]\) for a call are rejected outright: no \(\sigma\) could ever produce them, which usually means a stale or bad quote.

Plotting \(\sigma_{\text{implied}}\) against strike for a real option chain (panel 4 above) produces the famous volatility smile/skew: a curve, not a flat line. That shape is direct, visible evidence that the constant-\(\sigma\) assumption this whole dashboard otherwise relies on does not hold in real markets.

Model assumptions & limitations

Every number in this dashboard is only as good as the assumptions behind it. Being explicit about where a model is known to break is as important as the model itself:

  • Constant volatility. Both the CRR tree and Black–Scholes assume one \(\sigma\) for the whole life of the option. Panel 4 shows this is false for real markets — implied volatility varies by strike (the smile/skew) and, in reality, by expiry too (the full "volatility surface").
  • No dividends. The stock is assumed to pay nothing before expiry. With dividends, the "American call = European call" result checked in smoke_test.py can actually break: early exercise can become optimal just before an ex-dividend date.
  • Frictionless markets. No transaction costs, no bid-ask spread, unlimited borrowing/lending at the same risk-free rate, and infinite liquidity at every price. Panel 4's real option chain shows actual bid/ask spreads and volume/open-interest, i.e. exactly the frictions this model ignores.
  • Continuous, costless hedging (Black–Scholes specifically). The closed-form price assumes you can rebalance the replicating portfolio continuously and for free. The CRR tree only rebalances at N discrete steps — part of why it differs from Black–Scholes at finite \(N\) (the sawtooth in panel 2).
  • Historical vs. implied volatility mismatch. The "Fetch live data" button in panel 4 fills \(\sigma\) with historical (backward-looking, realised) volatility, while the smile shows implied (forward-looking, market-priced) volatility at each strike. They are rarely equal — comparing them is itself a real, if simple, volatility-trading signal.
